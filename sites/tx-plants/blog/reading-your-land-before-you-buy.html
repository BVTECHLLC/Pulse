Reading a Piece of Land Before You Buy It

The land tells you what it is before the listing does — in its weeds, its trees, its low spots, and its fence lines. Here's how I walk a property before money changes hands.

J By Jordan Polasek · July 26, 2026 · El Campo, TX

Every week someone asks me some version of “we're looking at land — what should we check?” The honest answer is that the land itself will tell you nearly everything, for free, if you know how to listen. Here's the walk I do before I'd ever talk numbers.

Read the trees first

Trees are a 50-year soil report. Big healthy pecans or oaks mean deep soil and water within reach of roots. Nothing but mesquite and hackberry means thin, droughty ground that's been grazed hard. Willows and cypress mark the wet spots more honestly than any survey. A property with no mature trees at all is either new caliche or old cropland — ask which.

Read the weeds like a lab test

Weeds are indicator species. Curly dock and sedges say “water stands here.” Purslane and plantain say compacted but workable. Lush careless weeds and pigweed taller than your truck bed say fertility — old corrals and pens grow the best weeds on the place, and that's exactly where the garden wants to be.

Walk it after a rain if you possibly can

One walk in the 48 hours after a good rain is worth five in dry weather. You'll see where water runs, where it stands, where the driveway will fail, and whether that “seasonal creek” on the listing is an asset or a liability. Standing water that's still there two days later is a pond site or a problem — decide which before you sign.

The boring paperwork that decides everything

  • Water — well depth and gallons-per-minute on neighboring properties; the county's groundwater district will talk to you.
  • What touches the land — easements, pipelines, and who maintains the road. A pipeline easement through your best soil rewrites your whole plan.
  • Restrictions and ag exemption — can you keep animals, build a barn, sell from a stand? Is the ag valuation transferable, and what does keeping it require?

Then match the plan to the land

The mistake is buying land and imposing a plan on it. The better way is the reverse: the wet corner is the elderberry patch, the deep bottomland is the orchard, the thin ridge is for natives that want to be dry. When the plan comes from the land, everything after gets easier and cheaper. The Almanac's best-use-per-acre guide picks this up from here.
