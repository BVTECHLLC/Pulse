Farm Equipment Repair Basics: Fix It Yourself

Equipment always breaks at the worst time. Learning to diagnose and repair your own gear saves money and downtime. Here's the mindset and the basics that cover most fixes.

J By Jordan Polasek · 10 min read · El Campo, TX

Anything with moving parts will eventually break, usually at the worst possible moment — the middle of harvest, the hottest day, the one weekend you have to get a job done. Learning to fix your own equipment is one of the highest-return skills on a homestead or farm. Most repairs aren't mysterious; they're systematic. Here's how I approach it.

The diagnostic mindset

Before touching a wrench, think like a doctor: what changed, and what are the symptoms? Most machines fail in predictable ways, and most problems trace back to a short list of usual suspects. Don't start replacing parts at random — that's how you spend money without fixing anything.

1

Observe

What exactly is it doing or not doing? When did it start? Any sounds, smells, smoke, leaks?

2

Check the basics first

The vast majority of 'won't start' problems are fuel, air, spark, or a dead battery. Check the cheap, easy things before the expensive, hard ones.

3

Isolate

Narrow it down system by system. Is it electrical? Fuel? Mechanical? Hydraulic? Test one thing at a time.

4

Source the part

Find the right part — model and serial number matter. Aftermarket is often fine and far cheaper.

5

Fix and document

Make the repair, then write down what it was and what fixed it. Next time it'll be five minutes, not five hours.

Small engines: the four things to check

Most small engines — mowers, tillers, generators, pumps — that won't run are failing on one of four fronts. Work through them in order:

  1. Fuel — is there fresh fuel reaching the carburetor? Old, stale gas is the single most common cause of a no-start.
  2. Air — is the air filter clogged? An engine needs to breathe.
  3. Spark — is the spark plug firing? Pull it, check the gap, look for fouling.
  4. Compression — if fuel, air, and spark are all good and it still won't run, you may have an internal mechanical issue.
Jordan’s tipStale fuel is the number-one killer of small engines. Ethanol-blended gas absorbs water and gums up carburetors within months. Run equipment dry before storage, use fuel stabilizer, and buy fuel fresh. Half the 'broken' mowers in America just have varnished carburetors from old gas.

Build a basic kit

You don't need a professional shop. A solid socket and wrench set, screwdrivers, pliers, a multimeter for electrical diagnosis, a spark plug socket, a grease gun, and the service manual for your specific machines will handle the large majority of repairs. The manual is the cheapest, most valuable tool you'll own — most are findable online for any model.

Know your limits

Some jobs — hydraulics under pressure, PTO shafts, anything that can store energy or crush you — are genuinely dangerous and worth paying a pro for, or at least learning properly before attempting. Knowing the line between 'I can fix this' and 'this can kill me' is part of the skill. There's no shame in calling someone for the dangerous ten percent.
