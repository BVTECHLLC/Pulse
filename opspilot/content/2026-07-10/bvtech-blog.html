Halfway through 2026, the pattern is clear: attackers have stopped treating small businesses as too small to bother with. They treat them as the easiest way in. At BVTech, we watch this play out every week across our clients in Sugar Land, Houston, Austin, and San Antonio — and the good news is that almost every incident we see would have been stopped by controls that cost less than a single day of downtime.

Here is what is actually hitting Texas small businesses right now, and exactly what to do about each one.

1. AI-written phishing that sounds like your bookkeeper

The clumsy, typo-ridden phishing email is gone. In its place: clean, well-written messages that reference real projects, real vendors, and real names pulled from your public footprint. We are seeing invoices "from" known suppliers with the bank details quietly changed, and "the owner" texting an employee to buy gift cards during a meeting.

The fix: a hard rule that any change to payment details or any urgent money request gets verified by a phone call to a known number — never a number in the email. Pair that with email filtering that flags look-alike domains. It is a five-minute policy that stops the single most expensive attack we see.

2. Ransomware that steals first, encrypts second

Modern ransomware crews copy your data before they lock it, so "we have good backups" is no longer the whole answer. They threaten to leak client records, tax documents, and contracts unless you pay. For a professional services firm, the reputational hit can be worse than the outage.

The fix: offline, tested backups remain non-negotiable — but add tight limits on who can reach what. Most ransomware spreads because one compromised login could touch every file on the network. Segment access, and a break-in stays a break-in instead of becoming a company-wide event.

3. MFA fatigue and the stolen session

Multi-factor authentication is working so well that attackers have pivoted to going around it — spamming your phone with approval prompts at 2 a.m. until someone taps "yes," or stealing the browser session token after you have already logged in.

The fix: move from tap-to-approve push notifications to number-matching or hardware keys, and shorten session lifetimes on anything sensitive. If you are on Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, these are settings you already own — they just have to be turned on correctly.

4. The unpatched edge device

Firewalls, VPN appliances, and remote-access tools are now a favorite entry point because they sit exposed to the internet and often go months without updates. A single out-of-date VPN box has quietly handed over entire networks.

The fix: every internet-facing device needs an owner, a patch schedule, and monitoring. If you cannot say when your firewall firmware was last updated, that is the finding — and it is a fixable one.

5. The vendor you forgot you connected

Third-party apps with standing access to your email, files, or accounting are the supply-chain risk hiding in plain sight. When one of them is breached, the attacker inherits whatever permissions you granted — often years ago, for a tool nobody uses anymore.

The fix: review connected apps quarterly and revoke anything you do not actively use. Least privilege is not just for employees; it applies to software too.

The through-line

None of these attacks are exotic. They succeed because of small gaps — a setting left at its default, a device nobody owns, a payment process built on trust instead of verification. That is genuinely encouraging, because gaps can be closed. You do not need a bigger budget than your competitors; you need the basics done deliberately and monitored continuously.
