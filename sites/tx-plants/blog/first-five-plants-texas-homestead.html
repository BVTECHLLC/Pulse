The First Five Plants I'd Put on Any Texas Homestead

If I had bare land and one season to start feeding my family from it, these are the five perennials I'd plant first — and exactly why each one earns its spot.

J By Jordan Polasek · July 27, 2026 · El Campo, TX

New homesteaders almost always start with a vegetable garden, and vegetable gardens are great — but annuals make you re-earn every calorie every single year. The smarter first move on new land is perennials: plant once, harvest for decades. If I were starting from a bare acre in Texas tomorrow, these five go in the ground before anything else.

1. Pecan — the hundred-year pantry

The pecan is the state tree for a reason: a mature pair of trees drops more protein and fat every fall than any garden bed you'll ever plant, and it does it for a century. Plant two compatible cultivars for pollination, give them your deepest soil, and they'll outlive your grandchildren.

2. Fig — fruit in year two, forever after

Nothing pays back faster. A fig cutting rooted this winter is handing you fruit by the second summer, shrugs at heat, and propagates for free — one tree becomes a fence line of them in a few seasons.

3. Elderberry — medicine on a fence line

Elderberry roots from a bare winter stick, thrives in the wet corner where nothing else will, and gives you immune-syrup berries plus elderflowers every year. Cook the berries — always — and it's one of the most useful plants on any homestead.

4. Sunchoke — the calorie bank you can't kill

Sunchokes are the closest thing to a guaranteed crop in Texas: a native sunflower that stores its calories underground, survives drought, and comes back harder every year. Corral them in their own bed — they mean it.

5. Maypop — the native fruit nobody plants

The maypop is our native passionfruit: an unkillable vine with an absurdly beautiful flower, real fruit, and calming-tea leaves. It dies to the ground each winter and returns from the root — zero care, honest returns.

The pattern

Notice what all five share: they're adapted to heat, they propagate cheap or free, and they compound — each year they give more for less work. That's the whole homesteading thesis in one planting plan. Browse the plant database for the full grow guide on each, and start with whichever your soil and water say you can support.
