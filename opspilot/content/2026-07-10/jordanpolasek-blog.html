I spend most of my week inside other people's technology — networks, inboxes, the quiet places where small businesses keep the things that keep them running. So when people ask me what is actually changing in IT right now, I do not answer from a trend report. I answer from what I am seeing in the field. Here are my mid-2026 field notes.

AI stopped being a demo and became plumbing

A year ago, "AI" in a small business meant someone experimenting with a chatbot. Now it is quietly running underneath things — drafting the first version of a proposal, summarizing a support ticket, catching an anomaly in the books before a human would. The businesses winning with it are not the ones chasing the flashiest tool. They are the ones who picked one boring, repetitive process and let software carry it, then moved to the next one.

My advice has not changed: do not adopt AI because it is the topic. Adopt it where you can name the hours it gives back. If you cannot point to the specific task it replaces, you are buying a subscription, not a result.

The "IT guy" model is finally breaking

For twenty years the default was one overloaded generalist — internal or a one-person shop — holding everything together with heroics and memory. That model is cracking, and honestly it should. The surface area is too big now. Security alone is a full-time discipline. So is compliance. So is keeping a fleet of laptops patched.

What is replacing it is not "hire a bigger department." It is systems: standardized setups, automation doing the repetitive work, and a team that documents everything so the business is not one resignation away from chaos. The goal is boring, and boring is the point.

Security became a business conversation, not a technical one

The most encouraging shift this year: owners are asking me about risk in plain business terms. Not "what firewall should I buy," but "what happens to my company if this goes wrong, and how much does it cost to make that less likely." That is the right question. Technology is downstream of it.

Speed is the new moat

The businesses pulling ahead are not the ones with the biggest tech budgets. They are the ones who can decide and move — turn on a new tool in a day, onboard a hire in an hour, recover from a bad day without it becoming a bad week. Good systems buy you speed. Speed compounds.

What I am telling people to do

Pick the one thing that would hurt most if it failed tomorrow — your data, your email, your ability to get paid — and make it boringly reliable before you chase anything new. Then do it again next quarter. That is the whole strategy. It is not glamorous, and it works.
